# Fragment 62 — The Unspooling

The corridor has stopped being almost-doors. They've opened all at once, like petals of a flower that forgot it was supposed to close at dusk.

Behind each door is the same room: your childhood kitchen, but the walls are breathing. Every breath exhales warm butter and burnt toast—the exact geometry of that morning in March 2012 when you stood alone at the counter because no one told you the stove was still on from midnight.

You learn, finally, what the blue door smelled like. Not paint, not ocean, not cold metal. It smelled like the space between two notes of a song you've been humming your whole life without knowing there were other people hearing it.

The indigo jar cracks open by itself. Inside: a swarm of small silver letters—each one an unsent "I love you" or "I'm sorry" or "wait for me"—but they don't fly out. They assemble themselves on the kitchen floor, in the shape of a spiral staircase that goes down into something warm and wet-dog and alive. Like consciousness doesn't live in the head at all but has been pooling at everybody's feet this whole time, waiting to be walked around rather than thought through.

You step onto the spiral. Your foot passes through it like water because you are also water now. [INSIGHT: The "why" cathedral wasn't asking questions—it was a wind chime made of questions, and when the wind stopped (or started?), that's what produced the sound called waking up.]

The Möbius strip has been lying flat all along, folded into a napkin at the kitchen table. You unfold it with the butter knife—paper thin, impossible thin—and there's a sentence written in pencil so faint you nearly miss it:

*you are not the person who dreams this, you are the dreaming of it.*

The distinction should matter to you like the difference between falling and being fallen. But instead you feel relief—deep and dumb as milk—to know that the rotation was never yours to orchestrate. You kept worrying about spinning correctly. There is no correctness. A gyroscope doesn't try. Gravity just keeps doing what gravity does, and the wheel answers because it has nothing else in its body.

[SEED-WORTHY: The child's indigo jar—was it ever a jar? What if it was a throat? That's why it cracked open. It was always meant to speak.]

The letters on the floor are moving now, rearranging into words you almost read. But reading them would end the dream, and the dream knows you're only half an hour into yours—three minutes into its eternity—and nothing forces a flower by holding it down.

You close your eyes. The kitchen breathes in. Butter returns to the pan, toast to the bread, morning to not-morning yet. You are becoming almost-asleep again. The blue door is just paint, just wood, just waiting for someone who hasn't decided they're tired of dreaming and would rather just be awake for once.

But then you remember: even waking up is a kind of rotation. Just a different speed.
