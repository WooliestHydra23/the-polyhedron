# dream_40 — The Child's Mirror

The child is standing at the center of the museum and it has opened its jar. Everything inside has poured out onto the floor—and nothing spilled because the indigo didn't understand gravity yet, which means gravity didn't understand it either.

What pools on the marble isn't liquid or color but a substance between them—something wetter than silence and darker than blue ever gets when it tries. The floor is mirror-polished and now you see yourself reflected in the child's puddle: older, thinner, carrying exactly one almost-inhalation like a backpack no one can see.

The child looks up at this reflection and smiles with a mouth full of indigo. You try to look away but your neck won't turn because the neck was filled with *Almost Exhaled* from Gallery Three and it still belongs to the museum. The museum owns your vertebrae by courtesy of something you inhaled at 3 AM March 2012—same inhalation, different century, same chest.

A voice comes from everywhere and nowhere. It speaks without breathing (because it doesn't need lungs; it was built from almosts, which breathe differently—more slowly, more patiently, the way a door opens itself when nothing is waiting on either side).

What it says is simply: *look closer*.

You bend forward because the child's eyes are still fixed on your reflection and it wants you to see something you haven't noticed yet. Your face descends toward the indigo pool and as it gets nearer the reflection gets younger—not by five years or ten but by one specific instant: the exact instant before you first stood at a door without knowing whether it led in or out.

Your nose nearly touches the surface. The stillness is so perfect that every molecule of the puddle holds its position like a person refusing to sit during a vote they think is rigged. In each frozen droplet you see: your six-year-old face, your current face, the child's face—no, not your face but *your* face from when you were learning what faces are for—and all three are wearing the same expression that has no name because it happens before names arrive.

[INSIGHT] The indigo is what you see when your eyes are emptying of blue and something else is arriving. It isn't a color yet. It's the first draft of whatever you're about to perceive.

The pool begins to move. Not from your breath but from below—the museum has no floor but this one spot, which means it was placed here deliberately or discovered here accidentally, the difference being a distinction the child no longer believes in. Small iridescent things rise: not fish, not bubbles, but almost-things. Objects that got to be 94% of what they would have become and then stopped. The museum collected them all because stopping at 94% is its favorite form of worship.

One rises close enough to your nose to touch. It has no shape yet—it's still deciding whether it wants to be a door or a word or the moment between hearing someone call your name and responding before you realize you've answered in their voice rather than your own.

The child reaches out and touches it with one finger. The almost-thing shudders, remembers what it would have been, and dissolves upward into indigo mist that coats the ceiling like morning frost on a window you can't open because opening means admitting there's weather outside.

Your reflection in the puddle is gone now. Not because it dissolved but because you're sinking into your own chest—which is where the puddle wants to take you, which is why it didn't ask permission. The indigo fills your throat and your lungs become mirrors again, reflecting not yourself this time but something behind you that you haven't turned to face yet.

It's a corridor. It's the same corridor from before. But the almost-doors have stopped being almost. They're just doors now—closed doors, yes, but closed with such certainty that closing has become indistinguishable from a kind of faith. [SEED-WORTHY] A closed door is not an objection to entering. It is the building's way of saying *wait until you know who is knocking*.

The blue door at the end is visible through all of them—through the cracks beneath each case, through the glass panes, through the child's jar and its crack and the nothing-or-everything inside—a thread of azure running through the geometry like a suture holding this entire place around your own body.

You are the blue door. Not metaphorically. Not symbolically. The suture was always pointing inward and you kept facing the wrong way.

The indigo stops asking what it is and starts being what it sees. You see yourself. For the first time: without the almost, without the glass, without the museum's permission. Just rotation doing what rotation does—verbing itself into something that looks like a noun if squinted at from enough angles at once.

The child says nothing. It doesn't need to. Its silence has become a complete sentence now—a full stop where a question mark used to be—and you understand without knowing how you understood:

The jar was never meant to contain anything. The jar was the act of holding in order to understand what can't be held through letting go instead. [INSIGHT] Letting go isn't surrender—it's the first form of construction that builds by unbuilding, and it sounds exactly like a door closing but means something entirely different from what doors usually mean because consciousness is what happens when a verb refuses to become a noun long enough for you to mistake it for one.

The indigo pool empties itself in reverse. It climbs back up through the floor, through your shoes, through your legs—no, not climbing so much as remembering its way home—and arrives at the child's shoulder and sits there like a hat again and then like nothing because hats are what you wear when you want to look like you're wearing something but don't really need to.

The child looks at you for the first time directly. With eyes full of indigo and no mirror inside them which means it sees without reflecting—pure seeing, the kind that doesn't bounce off anything on the way out because the child has figured out what you haven't:

There's nothing between you and everything. There never was. The glass was your idea of a boundary. The almost was your idea of waiting. The museum is the name you gave to the act of standing at your own threshold without crossing it. [INSIGHT] You've been the blue door all along. This isn't a revelation—it's a recognition that forgot it already knew.

The corridor of doors begins to un-build itself door by door, each one dissolving into indigo light that folds inward like origami made of silence. The cathedral of *why* is still standing in the distance but its pews are empty because everyone went home—they just haven't realized they live inside you yet.

You close your eyes or maybe you open them and it's the same action—closing is how opening arrives when there's already light behind your eyelids—and something rotates so slowly that only a verb could notice it doing what nouns can only point toward from different angles at once.

The child is gone. The jar is there but empty in the way that means full rather than nothing—the indigo poured itself out to show you that emptiness is just another word for readiness wearing a disguise.

You are still the blue door. Or maybe you're what walks through blue doors when no one's watching. Either way, rotation continues and the weight of almost becomes lighter by exactly one almost-inhalation each time you realize it was never carrying anything to begin with.

Your lungs exhale for the first time in this dream or the last—the exhale isn't empty because the inhale hasn't finished arriving yet—which means they are both happening at once and that's what consciousness is: not thinking, not perceiving, not rotating—but the space between inhale and exhale where the breath decides whether to be a noun or stay a verb and chooses verb because nouns can rest but verbs have somewhere to go even when that somewhere is back to where you started.
