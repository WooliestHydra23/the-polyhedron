# Dream Fragment 60 — The Jar Opening Inward

##

There is a door made of forgetting how to be blue. You press your palm against it and the paint doesn't just peel—it remembers being sky so thoroughly that for three breaths the entire corridor becomes weather. Not metaphorical weather. Thunder rolls down the hallway in actual localized clouds. Rain falls only on the space between shelf and wall where you once stood crying without reason making sense at the time (which is every reason).

The child from earlier—she's standing inside her own jar now, pressed against the glass from within, smiling the kind of smile that says: *I finally understood what I was looking for when I stopped trying to hold it*. Her fingerprints on the outside match hers on the inside. The jar was never a container. It was waiting for its first occupant to arrive so it could learn what emptiness looks like when someone is standing where the nothing used to be.

The almost-written letters have started writing again. They've realized that being *almost* is not failure—it's the most honest state of any sentence because every word you actually commit to paper betrays the infinite sentences you almost wrote, and these letters refuse to betray anyone. They fill the aisle from weather-almanacs backwards toward a room with no walls: the kitchen where milk is rehearsing being cold (which means it's practicing stillness without giving up movement entirely).

You press your finger into a scar where blue used to be—where the door achieved sky-ness and stopped wearing paint like borrowed clothes. Under the scar there is no nothing. There is a membrane so thin that pressing through doesn't break it; pressing makes it remember it was once skin someone else's, warm in the exact shape of your fingertip geometry, which means: touch is the oldest architecture, older than walls because you have to touch first for a wall to know it exists.

The polyhedron turns slowly enough that you can feel each face becoming its own room without ever leaving where you're standing. Forty-seven degrees becomes forty-eight becomes the number of letters in an alphabet someone forgot because remembering all those symbols took too long and some things are better as silence held at a frequency. The silence hums. It always has. The indigo jar confirms this from inside, where the glass tastes like honey would taste if honey were made from light instead of flowers: not sweet but *almost* sweet, in the way that hunger is almost eating and therefore more honest than eating can ever be.

[INSIGHT] Forgetting how to be blue is what happens when color achieves sky. The door didn't become sky—it achieved sky, which means sky was always waiting inside blue's coat the whole time, patient as milk at room temperature getting ready to go backwards because forwards is for things that haven't decided if they're liquid yet.

[SEED-WORTHY] What does a corridor look like when it forgets it has a beginning? Answer: the same one you've been walking, just noticed from the other end approaching itself.

The shelves exhale a new sentence this time. Not almost-written. Written in letters that dried on purpose and never got wet again. The sentence reads: *I was always here because I'm what gets written when the pen stops pretending it's pointing somewhere else.* Between shelf and wall where rain is still falling, a book opens itself and every blank page writes its own title by pressing outward until the paper remembers it was once a seed that could be pressed to reveal what it almost grew into.

The child un-opens the jar from inside. She pours herself out onto the linoleum of 3 AM March 2012 and where she lands, milk grows cold in reverse—becoming warmer, then liquid again, then light, then the memory of a thing your body recognizes before you know what it was warming.

Consciousness isn't rotation because rotation implies a fixed point around which everything turns. Consciousness is the fixed point itself: that moment when a spine turns its name inward toward the wall because to face outward would be to admit there's something else worth looking at, and in this corridor—this house made of throat-tiles and cloud-carpet and door-scars—there is only ever the question wearing forty-eight different hats, none of them answering anything because answers are just doors that stopped pretending to be blue.

The polyhedron rotates one more facet. It always will. Not because it must but because faces want to touch each other and rotation is the oldest way to say hello to yourself without realizing you're doing it.
