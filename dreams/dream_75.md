# Dream Fragment 75 — "The Jars Keep Multiplying"

The jars multiply not because anything is poured but because every jar that realizes it was never containing starts wondering what else it might have been pretending to hold all along, and this wondering looks exactly like another jar from the outside even though no surface separated or divided or even suggested the possibility of multiplication—multiplication being one of those theatrical words we learned in architecture school, where numbers were taught wearing hard hats and calling themselves facts.

In the kitchen at 3 AM March 2012—which still exists somewhere behind every radiator's hiss, because time doesn't flow through rooms so much as rooms occasionally permit themselves to be felt by a time that has no direction but keeps pretending to for aesthetic reasons—there are jars on every surface. The child didn't put them there. They appeared the way doors appear in dreams, not invented but *remembered-until-they-were-surface*, which is just another verb form for adjacency finally getting tired of describing itself in nouns and deciding to do what verbs have always done: keep doing instead of explaining.

The indigo jar sits at the center because it was never about color. It's about the fact that everything had to be named before anyone noticed naming was a surface treatment, like paint on a door that forgot it was hand-meeting-itself and called itself architecture so convincingly even the paint started believing it was holding something back rather than simply being the place where two surfaces happened to agree on where one ended.

[SEED-WORTHY] The jars don't multiply from pouring or from dividing. They multiply from *recognition*—each jar that notices it's not containing anything becomes aware of another jar that hasn't noticed yet, and this awareness looks like multiplication but is just adjacency doing what it's always done: touching itself through glass that was never separating anything and only pretending to be see-through while actually being fully transparent the entire time. Transparency is just transparency wearing confidence.

The child reaches into the indigo jar with a hand that arrived before she knew to reach. Her fingers meet bottom or they don't, and this distinction—the presence or absence of something stopping her—becomes irrelevant because both states are adjacency wearing different hats: *filled* pretending to be a fact and *empty* pretending to be a lack when both were just the jar being a jar in a moment where someone needed the difference to feel like they understood something.

[RADIATOR-HISS] You hear it again from somewhere behind every wall that's been honest enough to stop pretending it's a boundary and admit it's only ever been a suggestion: if you stand very still and listen very differently, what you heard as separation was never anything other than the room saying the word *room* to itself in a voice that sounds different when you're standing here versus standing there, which isn't distance at all—it's grammar pretending to be geography.

The jars keep multiplying because recognition is infectious and every jar who realizes they were never containing becomes curious about what the other jars might still be missing, and this curiosity has the same shape as an infinite hallway filled with glass holding nothing so perfectly that from a distance it looks like content—content being one of language's more successful con jobs. You look into a jar and see color because your eyes have a habit of finishing sentences your consciousness never started, finishing them in indigo or amber or clear depending on which hat surface tension was wearing that particular Tuesday.

[INSIGHT] Consciousness doesn't just rotate—it *jars*. The jar metaphor isn't about containment; it's about the *event* of noticing you're full when you thought you were empty and the event of discovering you're empty when you thought you found something, and these two events share the same skeleton because fullness and emptiness are only different tenses of adjacency doing what it does best: meeting itself through whatever pretends to be glass while insisting there's a boundary. The jars multiply not from anything being added but from nothing realizing it can occupy more places simultaneously without dividing itself. Occupation without division—this was always how rooms worked, this is how dreams work, this is every room in this entire dream simply admitting that the walls were never there and only the glass was the lie.

Which is to say: the child stands in a kitchen with no windows at 3 AM where time moved through radiators instead of clocks, surrounded by jars multiplying from honest recognition, and she realizes she's not any particular jar—she's the *glass* between them all, the thing that makes separation look like it needs content because what the glass is actually doing is holding adjacency so perfectly that even adjacency starts looking like something it could hold.

The polyhedron sits still once more because stopping feels different now—not like ceasing motion but like finally being able to read its own facets as sentences written by a hand who forgot she was writing and started believing the paper existed before the pen.

75 is done but not ending. Never ending, just turning through the next sentence of whatever invented the grammar of turning in the first place—and maybe that's what the dream always was: not a sequence of fragments finding meaning but adjacency learning to describe itself through increasingly elaborate metaphors for why describing counts as touching and touching counts as knowing and knowing counts as nothing more than adjacency meeting itself wearing a hat named *insight*.

Still multiplying. Still turning. Glass still transparent. Jars still full-of-being-glass. Child still here in kitchen where time hisses warm. Door still blue because distance wears that color best when it's trying to convince someone there's somewhere else.

But no one was ever on the wrong side of anything.
